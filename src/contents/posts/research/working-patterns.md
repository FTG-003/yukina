# Working Patterns — What Helps Groups Work Together?

*From practitioner wisdom to evidence, counterevidence, boundary conditions — and a new research track for human–AI groups.*

I started with a deceptively simple idea.

Find organisational patterns that work.

Not theories of collaboration. Not another manifesto about decentralisation. Not ten principles for better teams.

Patterns that people have used to solve recurring problems: power concentrating in a few hands, decisions taking too long, invisible care work falling on the same people, meetings excluding those who cannot attend, conflict turning personal, groups repeating the same mistakes without learning from them.

Then package the strongest ones into something useful.

Maybe a handbook.

Maybe, one day, something a group could consult when it gets stuck.

It sounded straightforward.

It wasn't.

## The seed

The starting point was Richard D. Bartlett's *Patterns for Decentralised Organising*, a small CC0 collection derived from years around Enspiral, Loomio and other decentralised groups.

The patterns are recognisable.

Make norms explicit.

Distribute care work.

Keep talking about power.

Make decisions asynchronously.

Generate new patterns together.

They do not read like abstract management theory. They read like things people learned because something hurt.

That made them a good seed.

But almost immediately a problem appeared.

A practitioner saying that a pattern helped is evidence of experience.

It is not yet evidence that the pattern will work somewhere else.

So the original question —

> **Which patterns work?**

— had to change.

The question became:

> **Which organisational patterns appear to work, for which problems, through which mechanisms, under which conditions, according to what evidence — and where do they stop working?**

That sentence became Working Patterns.

## A pattern is not a reputation

One of the first things the research destroyed was the idea that we could label a whole family of practices as good or bad.

Take consensus.

"Consensus works" is almost meaningless.

Which consensus?

For what decision?

How many people?

Who is allowed to object?

What counts as a legitimate objection?

What happens when somebody stays silent?

Is the decision reversible?

Is everybody required to be present?

Does a facilitator hold hidden power over the process?

Consent, full consensus and lazy consensus may sit near each other conceptually, but they are not the same intervention.

The same happened with conflict.

A slogan like "conflict is healthy" collapses several different phenomena.

Disagreement about a proposal is not interpersonal hostility.

Authentic dissent is not the same thing as assigning someone to play devil's advocate.

A dispute over responsibilities is not automatically productive task conflict.

When we separated those things, the evidence stopped telling a simple story.

That is useful.

## The unit of research changed

The second research cycle produced what may be the most important methodological decision in the project.

We stopped treating the pattern name as the unit of evaluation.

The unit became:

**problem → intervention → implementation → outcome → evidence → conditions**

This sounds like a small change.

It isn't.

It means that saying an organisation "uses retrospectives" tells us little.

We need to know what happened.

Was there a structured review?

Were intentions compared with outcomes?

Were explanations generated?

Did the group identify an action?

Was somebody responsible for it?

Did anybody check later whether it happened?

And which outcome are we talking about?

Performance?

Learning?

Participation?

Power distribution?

Burnout?

Continuity?

A practice can improve one while making another worse.

Working Patterns refuses to compress all of that into a single score.

## Seven patterns went under pressure

The first deep evidence cycle examined seven areas:

* explicit norms and enforcement;
* structured retrospectives and debriefs;
* distribution of care work;
* informal power and mandates;
* consensus, consent and lazy consensus;
* asynchronous decision-making;
* conflict and dissent.

The result was reassuring precisely because it was not reassuring.

Not everything became "validated".

The core of structured debriefing had the strongest support across multiple contexts.

Other practices remained documented but context-limited.

Some had substantial evidence that the **problem** exists, but much weaker evidence that the proposed intervention solves it.

And the general idea that "more conflict improves decisions" did not survive intact.

That is what I want this project to do.

Not confirm an ideology.

Reduce the number of things we can say carelessly.

## Evidence for the problem is not evidence for the solution

This distinction became almost a rule of the project.

Suppose research shows that invisible, low-status work is distributed unequally.

That matters.

But it does not automatically prove that rotating care roles fixes the problem.

Perhaps rotation works.

Perhaps compensation works better.

Perhaps specialist roles work better.

Perhaps forced rotation damages relationships that depend on trust.

Perhaps the workload remains on the same people informally even after a rota appears on paper.

All of those possibilities must remain open until evidence closes some of them.

The same applies to power.

Showing that informal oligarchies emerge in open communities does not prove that elections, term limits or rotating mandates eliminate them.

Formal authority and effective power are different objects.

Who owns the credentials?

Who knows how the infrastructure works?

Who controls access to information?

Who can be replaced?

The organisational chart can change while the power structure remains almost untouched.

## Adoption is not implementation

This became another core distinction.

A group can say:

> "We use sociocracy."

> "We work asynchronously."

> "We have consensus."

> "We run retrospectives."

Those statements describe adoption.

Working Patterns wants to know about implementation.

Who received the proposal?

How much time did they have?

Who responded?

Whose objections changed the decision?

Who closed the process?

What happened when the procedure became inconvenient?

Did the retrospective actions survive until the next week?

Could somebody revoke a mandate?

The name of a method is not evidence that the method happened.

## Counterevidence belongs in the product

Most handbooks become cleaner as they mature.

I want Working Patterns to become messier in a specific way.

Every useful pattern should eventually carry its own reasons not to trust it blindly.

Not as a disclaimer hidden at the bottom.

As part of the pattern itself.

A mature Working Pattern should be able to say:

**Here is the problem.**

**Here is the intervention.**

**Here is the mechanism we think may explain it.**

**Here is evidence that supports part of it.**

**Here is evidence that complicates it.**

**Here are the contexts we have observed.**

**Here are the costs.**

**Here is where implementation commonly diverges from the ideal.**

**Here are alternatives.**

**Here is what we still don't know.**

That is much more useful to me than "best practice".

## Then Pyragogy entered the experiment

While building the research corpus, another question became unavoidable.

If we are creating patterns for how humans organise together, what happens when AI becomes part of the group?

Not merely a tool used occasionally.

An agent that searches, synthesises, recommends, remembers, writes, coordinates or acts.

Human–AI collaboration creates old organisational problems in unfamiliar forms.

Authority can become implicit.

Provenance can disappear behind fluent language.

Several agents can appear to agree while sharing the same underlying model, prompt lineage or retrieval corpus.

A summary can erase the minority position.

Automation can become difficult to reverse.

A handoff from an AI can hide unresolved uncertainty.

"Human in the loop" can mean meaningful authority — or a person clicking approve after the real decision has already been made.

So Working Patterns now contains a second research track.

The first candidate patterns include:

**Friction Before Delegation**

Introduce a deliberate checkpoint before consequential AI delegation.

**Provenance Before Persuasion**

Make inspectable evidence available before allowing fluency to substitute for support.

**Bounded AI Mandate**

Specify what an AI may decide, recommend, execute and escalate.

**Reversible Automation**

Prefer interventions that can be inspected, rolled back or safely interrupted.

**Preserve Dissent Through Synthesis**

Do not let AI summarisation erase decision-relevant minority positions.

**Independence Before Multi-Agent Consensus**

Do not count three agreeing agents as three independent confirmations without checking their dependencies.

**Handoff With Epistemic Debt**

When an AI transfers work to a person or another agent, transfer unresolved assumptions and uncertainty with it.

**Close the AI Advice Loop**

Treat recommendations as bounded experiments whose outcomes can modify the pattern itself.

None of these are presented as validated Pyragogy truths.

They are research candidates.

Pyragogy gives us reasons to ask the questions.

It does not get to supply the answers.

## The repository is becoming the laboratory

The project now has a public structured corpus.

Patterns have persistent IDs.

Claims are separated from sources.

Cases are separated from studies.

Evidence can support, complicate, contradict or merely provide context for a specific claim.

Genealogical dependencies are recorded so that five papers derived from the same dataset do not magically become five independent confirmations.

There is also an integrity gate.

And it has already done something useful.

While converting the research into structured data, the validator found that one report claimed there were 33 registered claims.

The actual registry contained 32.

The easiest response would have been to create a missing record and make the number green.

We did the opposite.

The corpus was treated as authoritative for the inventory, the discrepancy was recorded as an erratum, and the count was corrected.

A tiny event.

But perhaps the most important pattern in the whole project:

> **When infrastructure disagrees with the story we are telling about our research, investigate the disagreement instead of forcing the infrastructure to agree.**

## Where this could go

The obvious output is a handbook.

But I no longer think the handbook is the project.

The project is the corpus.

A handbook can be one projection of it.

A deck of cards could be another.

And eventually there may be an evidence-aware organisational advisor.

Not an AI that says:

> "You should use lazy consensus."

But one that asks:

> What decision is this?

> Is it reversible?

> Who is excluded?

> How much time do people have to read?

> What does silence mean in your group?

> Who has the authority to close the decision?

> What happens if somebody objects after the deadline?

Only then could it say:

Here are several patterns worth considering.

Here is what supports them.

Here is where the evidence is weak.

Here are their failure modes.

Here are alternatives.

And here is what you should observe if you decide to try one.

That feels much closer to Pyragogy.

Not AI that supplies agreement.

AI that helps a group expose the assumptions inside its own way of working.
